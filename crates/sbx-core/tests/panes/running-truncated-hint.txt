  portable across different machine architectures. Existing high-level languages
  of the era, like FORTRAN and COBOL, were designed for numerical or business
  computing and were poorly suited to the kind of low-level manipulation of
  memory, bytes, and hardware registers that operating system code demanded.
  What was needed was a "systems programming language" — something that could be
  used to write the guts of an operating system, yet still be recompiled
  instead of rewritten when moving to new hardware.

  The Precursors: BCPL and B

  The lineage that led to C runs through two earlier languages. The first is
  BCPL (Basic Combined Programming Language), created by Martin Richards at
  Cambridge University in 1966. BCPL was itself designed as a simplified,
  portable systems language, stripped of most high-level abstractions like data
  types beyond the machine word. It was intentionally minimal, treating memory
  largely as an undifferentiated sequence of words that the programmer could
  manipulate directly — a philosophy that would echo forward into C's approach
  to pointers and memory.
